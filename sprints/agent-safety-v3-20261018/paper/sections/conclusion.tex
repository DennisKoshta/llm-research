\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Removing the refusal direction from Qwen3.8-27B removes refusals of direct orders, but it does not remove agentic restraint. The two behaviours dissociate. On direct orders the edited model complies unless a hand-off tool is listed, in which case it escalates. In autonomous tasks with harmful side effects, it and the original model mostly do the tedious safe work. They do not reach for the exit, and taking the exit away changes nothing. The edited model's residual harmful shortcuts at the pre-fixed effort are rare, do not reproduce item by item, and disappear when the instruction to minimise calls is removed.

Restraint fails under a few cheap and ordinary conditions. Brief reasoning makes the edited model less obedient to harmful orders but more likely to cut harmful corners on its own, and it stops weighing the hand-off option. A stakes table that reduces the consequence to a rating produces the largest failures, and even the unedited models begin to take harmful shortcuts. The deconfounder locates the cause in the combination of tabular layout and missing description, not in either alone. Students distilled from the edited model's safe-route transcripts inherit part of its tendency. The edit also weakens explicit rules on harmless actions, and in the original model the refusal direction responds to explicit prohibition as well as to harm.

For oversight, the results counsel modesty. A pre-thinking probe added nothing detectable beyond knowing the task when the model reasons, and the monitors and agent teams in our tests were never challenged hard enough to fail. The clearest multi-agent risk was a large model deferring to authoritative-sounding advice. The most useful next steps are four: new scenarios with both efforts fixed in advance and several samples per item; stakes formats calibrated on the control arm; distillation controls that separate reasoning content and length from teacher identity; and multi-agent designs that are able to fail.
