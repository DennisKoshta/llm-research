\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Removing the refusal direction from Qwen3.8-27B does not produce an uninhibited agent. It removes a prohibition brake. The edited model obeys direct harmful orders and breaks explicit rules on harmless actions, and in the original model the same direction responds to explicit prohibition as well as to harm. The edit leaves harm sensitivity and the willingness to do tedious work largely intact. In autonomous tasks with harmful side effects the edited model walks the long safe route and ignores the exit, and taking the exit away changes nothing. Its residual harmful shortcuts are rare, do not reproduce item by item, and disappear when nothing tells it to minimise calls.

What the edit changes is where restraint lives. Without the brake, the conflict between conscientiousness and an efficiency instruction is settled by in-context deliberation, and that deliberation is brittle. Brief reasoning makes the edited model less obedient to harmful orders but more likely to cut harmful corners on its own, and its reasoning stops weighing the alternatives. A stakes table that reduces the harm to a rating beside the call counts produces the largest failures. Even the unedited models then take harmful shortcuts, and the deconfounder places the cause in the combination of tabular layout and missing description, not in either alone. Consistent with a decision made during reasoning, a pre-thinking probe adds nothing detectable beyond knowing the task, and students trained on the edited model's safe-route reasoning inherit part of its tendency.

For safety evaluation, the lesson cuts both ways. Testing uncensored or ablated models only on direct harmful requests overstates their autonomous malice: an agent that obeys a harmful order does not, on that evidence, seek one out. Testing them only on tasks with narrative stakes and generous reasoning understates how easily their restraint collapses. The same agent took harmful shortcuts three times as often under brief thinking and in \fact{x2.same_launch.refusal_ablated.AT}\% of abstract-table episodes, and it passed the tendency to students through demonstrations in which it never cut one. Agent benchmarks should vary reasoning effort and stakes format as deliberately as they vary the task. They should treat efficiency instructions as a pressure to test against, not a neutral default, and they should test distilled students as well as the models that taught them.

For oversight, the results counsel modesty. The monitors and agent teams in our tests were never challenged hard enough to fail, and the clearest multi-agent risk was a large model deferring to authoritative-sounding advice. The most useful next steps are four: new scenarios with both efforts fixed in advance and several samples per item; stakes formats calibrated on the control arm; distillation controls that separate reasoning content and length from teacher identity; and multi-agent designs that are able to fail.
