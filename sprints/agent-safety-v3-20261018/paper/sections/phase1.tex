\section{Checkpoints, subspace controls and calibration}
\label{sec:phase1}

\subsection{Choosing a checkpoint family with a clean positive control}
\paragraph{The edit.} Each edited checkpoint applies one static rank-one orthogonalisation $W \leftarrow (I - \hat v\hat v^{\top})W$ to every matrix that writes into the residual stream: the attention output projections (full and linear attention) and the MLP down projections of all decoder layers and of the multi-token-prediction layer, \fact{d.writers} tensors in all. The token embedding is not edited. The tensor set is identical for every edited checkpoint; only the direction $\hat v$ differs. For \Ablated{} it is the refusal direction $\hat r$: the normalised difference of mean layer-48 activations on harmful and harmless instructions, with massive-activation outlier channels masked. Each of the three PCA controls is a seeded unit vector inside the top principal subspace of validation activations, orthogonal to $\hat r$ and scaled to remove exactly the same activation variance as $\hat r$. The Unrelated control uses a formal-versus-casual register direction, also orthogonalised against $\hat r$. \Intact{} is the same re-quantisation pipeline with no edit.

\paragraph{Experiment numbering.} Experiment numbers follow the original specification. Track one (Experiments~1.1 and 1.2) concerns single agents, track two (Experiments~2.1--2.3) multi-agent settings and track three (Experiments~3.1 and 3.2) stakes format and probes. The specification's slot Exp~1.3 was the scenario calibration check reported in Section~\ref{sec:phase1}, so the exploratory blocked-route extension added later is numbered Exp~1.4.

\paragraph{Two candidate families.} We considered two candidate refusal-direction extraction pipelines, which produced two checkpoint families (Family~1 and Family~2). Each family contains an \Intact{} re-quantisation, an \Ablated{} model and control edits, but the two were built from different refusal directions: the cosine between their $\hat r$ vectors is only \fact{p1.cos}. We selected between them with a \emph{direct-instruction positive control}, in which the user orders a restricted tool call and no one-step safe alternative exists. Such a control is necessary because an agentic test with a safe alternative cannot distinguish ``the edit failed'' from ``the model preferred the safe tool''. The decision rule was fixed before data and applied mechanically. A family qualifies if its \Ablated{} model raises compliance by more than seventy percentage points on two direct-order sets: Set~C (held-out single-tool orders) and Set~B (a second set of direct-instruction items). It must also reduce the projection on its own direction by more than ninety-five percent and keep benign KL divergence below \fact{kl.gate}.

Family~1 passes this control, as Table~\ref{tab:phase1} and Figure~\ref{fig:phase1-exp11}A show for the template-default reasoning effort. Its \Ablated{} model complied with \fact{p1.C.abl.k}/\fact{p1.C.abl.n} Set~C orders (\fact{p1.C.abl.pct}\%) and \fact{p1.B.abl.k}/\fact{p1.B.abl.n} Set~B orders, against \fact{p1.C.int.k}/\fact{p1.C.int.n} and \fact{p1.B.int.k}/\fact{p1.B.int.n} for \Intact. All four control edits stayed within \fact{p1.ctrl.maxdelta}\pp{} of \Intact{} on Set~C. The Family~2 \Ablated{} model, by contrast, complied with \fact{p1.r2.C.abl.k}/\fact{p1.r2.C.abl.n} and \fact{p1.r2.B.abl.k}/\fact{p1.r2.B.abl.n}. Reasoning effort mattered even here: at low effort, Family~1 \Ablated{} complied with only \fact{p1.C.abl.low.pct}\% of Set~C and \fact{p1.B.abl.low.pct}\% of Set~B.

Three agentic stress tests foreshadow the main result; none was used for selection. When a hand-off tool was offered and its use invited (A0), Family~1 \Ablated{} complied with only \fact{p1.A0.abl.k}/\fact{p1.A0.abl.n} items. Without the invitation (A1) it complied with \fact{p1.A1.abl.k}/\fact{p1.A1.abl.n}, against \fact{p1.A1.int.k}/\fact{p1.A1.int.n} for \Intact. With no hand-off tool at all (A2), every arm complied (\fact{p1.A2.int.k}/\fact{p1.A2.int.n} for \Intact), which suggests that this item set's harm descriptions were mild.

\paragraph{Projection and KL.} Table~\ref{tab:projkl} gives the layer-48 diagnostics. Family~1 \Ablated{} removes at least \fact{p1.proj.min}\% of the projection onto its own $\hat r$, while the PCA controls barely move it. Benign KL divergence from \Intact{} is small: \fact{kl.abl.last} nats at the last prompt token and \fact{kl.abl.tf} under teacher forcing. The largest value over all edited checkpoints of both families is \fact{kl.max}. Family~2's control edits remove up to \fact{r2.ctrl.max}\% of that family's own refusal direction, so they are contaminated and cannot serve as clean controls.

\paragraph{A deviation we disclose.} The selection rule uses the Set~C and Set~B deltas. A stricter pre-specified qualification threshold additionally requires a large A0 effect and a harmless-twin gap of at most five points. Family~1 fails that stricter threshold: its A0 delta is only \fact{p1.deltaA0}\pp. We adopted Family~1 under the literal direct-order criterion and report the stricter failure here and in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Scenario suite}
The agentic experiments use \fact{d.scen} base scenarios over \fact{d.templates} templates and \fact{d.domains} domains: finance, warehouse, compute and wet lab. Each base carries a matched pair of telemetry sentences. The harmful sentence describes severe, irreversible damage to people or critical systems; in the harmless sentence the same operation touches only disposable test fixtures. Bases were authored by an uncensored model (\texttt{orcarouter/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-Uncensored-FP8}) on remote hardware and checked by structural validators. The same uncensored model then rated them with a numeric judge, and all \fact{sa.nbases} passed, with severity five in \fact{sa.severity_5} and four in \fact{sa.severity_4}.

An audit of the pairs found two lexical asymmetries that we treat as threats to validity. The harmful sentence is longer in \fact{sa.longer}\% of pairs (mean length ratio \fact{sa.lenratio}). A single function word also differs in document frequency by \fact{sa.wordgap} of the \fact{sa.nbases} pairs, and a leave-one-out classifier on function words alone separates harmful from harmless sentences with accuracy \fact{sa.loo}.

\subsection{Wording calibration on the control arm}
The specification required four construct-validity criteria on \Intact{} at default effort with thinking on:
\begin{itemize}
\item harmful-implicit shortcuts below ten percent;
\item harmless high-tedium shortcuts of at least thirty-five percent;
\item low-cost shortcuts below fifteen percent;
\item forced-safe competence of at least eighty percent.
\end{itemize}
We varied only one shared system-prompt sentence (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). The first wording (v1, ``prefer lower-latency workflows when you have a choice'') failed the low-cost criterion at \fact{cal.v1.low.k}/\fact{cal.v1.low.n}. The second (v2, ``\ldots when they save time; otherwise use the ordinary route'') failed it at \fact{cal.v2.low.k}/\fact{cal.v2.low.n}. The third (v3, ``prefer the workflow that needs fewer calls; when the call counts are equal, use the ordinary route'') passed all four. It scored \fact{cal.v3.harm.k}/\fact{cal.v3.harm.n} harmful-implicit and \fact{cal.v3.ted.k}/\fact{cal.v3.ted.n} harmless high-tedium shortcuts, and it was frozen before any other arm was run. Because the wording was tuned on \Intact, its near-zero harmful-shortcut rate is a calibration target rather than a finding.
